The case begins

The Case of the Ordinary Morning

It is 7:02 a.m. You wake up. Your breath fogs the cold bedroom window. In the kitchen the kettle begins to sing. An ice cube bobs in your orange juice: floating, when by rights it should sink. A pinch of salt vanishes into the pan. The milk in the fridge is still fresh after a week, though it would have spoiled in a day on the counter.

You think nothing of it. You should. Each of these small events is a clue, and they all point to one hidden culprit. It has been at work in every object you have ever held, every meal you have eaten and every breath you have taken. It is too small to see, and you will meet it in disguise many times before you recognise it.

This is chemistry: the study of what things are made of and how they change. Your job, detective, is to work through nine case files, collect nine clues, and name the culprit at the end.

Three levels of seeing

From Melon to Atom

Every detective needs a magnifying glass. Ours zooms a billion times. Chemists look at the world on three levels, and the trick of this whole subject is learning to jump between them. The macro level is the melon in your hands: colour, taste, coldness. The micro level is what the melon is really made of: cells, then molecules, then atoms. The symbolic level is the shorthand chemists write it in, like H₂O.

Case File01

Particles · states of matter

The Restless Crowd

Around 400 BCE, a Greek thinker named Democritus picked up a piece of cheese (or so the story goes) and asked a strange question: if I keep cutting this in half, forever, what happens? He decided you must eventually reach a piece so small it cannot be cut. He called it atomos: “uncuttable.”

For two thousand years almost nobody believed him. You can't see atoms, after all. Then, in 1827, the botanist Robert Brown looked through a microscope at pollen grains floating in water and saw them jiggling, nervously and endlessly, for no visible reason. In 1905 a young patent clerk named Albert Einstein worked out why. The grains were being battered from every side by invisible water molecules, like a beach ball pushed around by an unseen crowd. He even calculated how big the molecules had to be. The jiggle was the first fingerprint of the culprit's world.

Here is the first great idea of chemistry: everything is made of tiny particles, and they never stop moving. What we call temperature is simply how fast they move. What decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas is a tug-of-war: motion pulls the particles apart, and attraction holds them together.

How small? A single drop of water contains about 1,700,000,000,000,000,000,000 molecules. If each one were a grain of sand, the drop would make enough sand to bury the whole of the Netherlands about 40 metres deep.

Everyday miracle

Why sweating keeps you alive

When you sweat, the fastest water molecules on your skin escape into the air first. The ones left behind are the slower, cooler ones. Your skin cools down every time a molecule leaves.

Evaporating just one litre of sweat removes about as much heat as it takes to bring a litre of cold water to the boil and then boil every drop of it away. Every hot day, you are being kept alive by a crowd of escaping molecules.

Case File02

Inside the atom

The Empty Room

Democritus said atoms couldn't be cut. In 1897 the physicist J.J. Thomson proved him wrong. Out of ordinary atoms he pulled tiny, negatively charged particles, nearly two thousand times lighter than the lightest atom. He called them electrons. Atoms had parts.

Thomson imagined the atom as a plum pudding: a soft ball of positive charge with electrons dotted through it like raisins. It was a cosy picture, and everyone liked it.

Then in 1909, in a darkened lab in Manchester, Ernest Rutherford and his students Geiger and Marsden fired tiny, fast, positively charged bullets (alpha particles) at a sheet of gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick. If atoms were soft puddings, every bullet should punch straight through. Make your prediction below before you fire.

Lab 2a · Famous experiment

Choose a prediction, then fire.

In the real experiment almost every particle sailed straight through, but about one in eight thousand bounced back. Rutherford was stunned: “It was quite the most incredible event that has ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you.”

The only explanation was that all of the atom's positive charge, and nearly all its mass, is crammed into a tiny core: the nucleus. The rest is almost completely empty, with electrons whirling far out. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be a pea on the centre spot.

Inside the nucleus are protons (positive charge) and neutrons (no charge). The number of protons is the atom's identity card. One proton is always hydrogen. Six is always carbon. Seventy-nine is always gold. Change the proton count and you have a different element.

Everyday miracle

You have never touched anything

When you rest your hand on a table, the outer electrons of your skin and the table come close, and because like charges repel, they push each other away. What you feel as touch is electric repulsion across a tiny gap.

You are, in a very real sense, always hovering a whisker above your chair.

Case File03

The periodic table

The Family Album

By the 1860s, chemists had found more than sixty elements and had no idea how they fitted together. In St Petersburg, a wild-bearded professor named Dmitri Mendeleev wrote each element on a card, with its weight and its habits, and played a kind of chemical solitaire, laying the cards out over and over.

In 1869 he saw the pattern. When he lined the elements up by weight, their personalities repeated, like the days of a week. He arranged them in rows so that elements with similar behaviour fell into the same column. His boldest move was to leave gaps. “These elements exist,” he declared, “we just haven't found them yet.” He even predicted their weights and colours.

Six years later a French chemist discovered gallium, and it filled one of Mendeleev's gaps almost exactly. Mendeleev wrote to tell him that his measured density was wrong. He was right: the discoverer re-measured and got Mendeleev's number.

Today we know why the pattern repeats. Elements in the same column have the same number of electrons in their outermost shell, and those outer electrons decide almost everything about how an atom behaves. The table is a family album. Each column is a family with a shared temperament.

Electrons per shell, from the inside out. The last number is the one that matters.

Everyday miracle

Fireworks are a periodic table in the sky

Heat an atom and its electrons jump to higher shells, then fall back and give off light of one exact colour. Each element has its own colour: strontium red, sodium yellow (like old street lamps), copper blue-green, barium green.

The same trick tells us what distant stars are made of. We read their light like a barcode.

Case File04

Chemical bonds

The Deal

Every atom envies the noble gases. Helium, neon and argon have perfectly full outer shells, and a full shell is a calm, low-energy state. For most atoms a full outer shell means eight electrons (the octet rule; for tiny hydrogen and helium it's two). Atoms will make deals to get there, and those deals are what we call chemical bonds.

Meet two of the most dangerous characters in the album. Sid (sodium) is a soft silver metal that bursts into flame in water. Clo (chlorine) is a choking green gas. Sid has one electron too many; Clo is one short. You can probably guess the deal.

Lab 4a · Ionic bond

Sid meets Clo

Sodium (2, 8, 1) and chlorine (2, 8, 7). Only the outer shells are drawn in full.

SID · sodium“Take it! Please. This lonely electron has been nothing but trouble.”
CLO · chlorine“Don't mind if I do. Eight at last. And now you're positive and I'm negative… so I suppose we're stuck together.”

That stickiness between a positive ion and a negative ion is an ionic bond. Billions upon billions of Sids and Clos stack into a perfect cube: a grain of table salt.

Not every deal is a handover. When two atoms are both hungry for electrons, neither will give one up, so they share. Two hydrogens share a pair of electrons and become H₂. Oxygen shares with two hydrogens and becomes water. A shared pair of electrons is a covalent bond, and it's what holds together almost every molecule in your body.

Carbon, with four outer electrons, can share in four directions at once. That makes it the master builder: chains, rings and branches, millions of different molecules. Arrangement matters enormously. Diamond and the graphite in your pencil are both pure carbon, joined in different patterns.

Everyday miracle

You season your food with an explosive and a poison

Sodium metal explodes in water. Chlorine gas was used as a weapon in the First World War. Yet once Sid hands over his electron, both become calm, stable ions, and together they're something your nerves need to fire every single thought.

The lesson is a huge one: the properties of a substance come from how its atoms are bonded, not just from which atoms are present.

Case File05

Polarity · water

The Bent One

Ola (oxygen) shares electrons with her two hydrogens, but she doesn't share fairly. Oxygen's nucleus has eight protons pulling on the shared electrons, and hydrogen's has one. The electrons spend more of their time near Ola. So the oxygen end of the molecule becomes slightly negative (δ−), and the hydrogen ends become slightly positive (δ+).

There's a twist. Water isn't a straight line. It's bent, at an angle of about 104.5°, like a boomerang or Mickey Mouse's head. That bend means one side of the molecule is negative and the other positive: a tiny magnet. We call such molecules polar.

The positive hydrogens of one water molecule are attracted to the negative oxygen of the next. These attractions are called hydrogen bonds. They are much weaker than real bonds, but there are astronomically many of them. They make water sticky, help it climb up trees, let insects walk on it, and pull salt crystals apart ion by ion.

Everyday miracle

Why fish survive the winter

When a lake freezes, the ice floats to the top and forms a lid that insulates the water below. The bottom stays at about 4 °C, the temperature at which liquid water is densest, and life carries on beneath the ice.

If ice sank, like nearly every other solid does in its own liquid, lakes and oceans would freeze from the bottom up, layer after layer. Life on Earth may owe its existence to one bent molecule.

Case File06

Chemical reactions

The Great Rearrangement

Paris, the 1770s. Antoine Lavoisier was rich, brilliant and obsessed with weighing things. While others watched what happened in their flasks, Lavoisier sealed his reactions in closed glass vessels and weighed everything, before and after, on the most precise balances in Europe.

Wood burned to ash. Metals turned to powder. Water boiled to vapour. But when nothing could escape, the total weight never changed. He summed it up in a sentence that still runs all of chemistry: “Nothing is lost, nothing is created, everything is transformed.”

A chemical reaction is a rearrangement. Bonds break, atoms swap partners, and new bonds form, but every atom that goes in comes out. A chemical equation is the recipe, and it must balance: the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.

Lavoisier's story ends darkly. He was also a tax collector, and during the French Revolution he was sent to the guillotine in 1794. The mathematician Lagrange said: “It took them only an instant to cut off that head, and a hundred years may not produce another like it.”

Everyday miracle

Trees are made of air

In the 1600s, Jan Baptist van Helmont planted a 2 kg willow in a pot of carefully weighed soil and gave it nothing but water. Five years later the tree weighed 76 kg, and the soil had lost only about 60 grams.

Where did the tree come from? Mostly from carbon dioxide in the air, rearranged with water using sunlight. A tree's trunk is air, rebuilt atom by atom. When you burn a log, those atoms simply return to the sky.

Case File07

Reaction rates

The Race Against Time

Knowing that a reaction can happen is only half the story. A diamond can turn into graphite, but it would take longer than the age of the universe. A firework explodes in a fraction of a second. So what controls the speed?

The answer is collision theory. Particles can only react if they bump into each other, and only if they hit hard enough to break old bonds. That minimum energy is called the activation energy: a hill every reaction must climb before it can roll down the other side.

So there are four ways to speed a reaction up. Heat it, so particles move faster and hit harder. Crowd it (raise the concentration), so they collide more often. Grind it (more surface area), so more particles are exposed. Or add a catalyst, a helper that offers a lower hill and isn't used up itself.

Everyday miracle

You are a factory of a trillion catalysts

Digesting a sandwich at body temperature without help would take decades. Your body uses enzymes: protein catalysts shaped like perfectly fitted locks. Some speed up their reaction more than a million billion times.

Right now, thousands of different enzyme-driven reactions are running in every one of your cells. It is the busiest chemistry lab in the known universe.

Case File08

Acids & bases

Sour & Slippery

Remember Hal, the hydrogen atom: one proton, one electron. Strip away his electron and what's left is a bare proton, H⁺. It's the smallest, most restless character in chemistry, and it is passed from molecule to molecule like a hot potato.

In 1884, a Swedish student named Svante Arrhenius suggested that some substances, when dissolved in water, break apart into charged pieces. His examiners were so unimpressed they gave his thesis the lowest passing grade. Nineteen years later the same idea won him the Nobel Prize.

Here's the modern version. An acid is a substance that gives away H⁺. Acids taste sour, like lemon, vinegar or cola. A base is a substance that takes H⁺, often using hydroxide ions (OH⁻). Bases taste bitter and feel slippery, like soap. Mix an acid and a base and they cancel: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. That's called neutralisation.

Chemists measure acidity with the pH scale, from 0 to 14. Pure water sits in the middle at 7. Below 7 is acidic, above 7 is basic. The catch is that each step is ten times stronger than the one before. Lemon juice, at pH 2, has 100,000 times more H⁺ than pure water.

Macro

Vinegar poured onto baking soda fizzes into a foam. Lemon makes your mouth pucker. Soap is slippery.

Micro

Acetic acid molecules in vinegar hand H⁺ ions to the bicarbonate in baking soda. The products include carbon dioxide gas, which makes the bubbles.

Symbolic
H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂O + CO₂↑

The ↑ arrow means a gas is escaping. pH = how many H⁺ ions, counted in powers of ten.

Everyday miracle

An acid bath inside you, and the knife-edge of your blood

Your stomach holds acid at about pH 1.5 to 2, strong enough to dissolve some metals. It survives because its lining constantly makes protective mucus and replaces its cells every few days.

Your blood, by contrast, must stay between pH 7.35 and 7.45. Even a small drift outside that range can be fatal. Chemical buffers in your blood soak up spare H⁺ or release it, second by second, for your whole life.

Case File09

Energy · the chemistry of life

The Carbon Odyssey

The last case follows one character on the longest journey in this whole file: Cass, a single carbon atom. Cass is billions of years old. She has been part of a star, a dinosaur's breath, a fern, and an ocean. And right now, quite possibly, she's part of you.

Her story is a story about energy. Breaking a bond always costs energy; forming a bond always releases it. When the new bonds in the products are stronger than the old bonds, the reaction gives out energy, usually as heat or light. We call that exothermic, like fire. When it's the other way round, the reaction soaks energy up. That's endothermic, like an instant cold pack.

Life runs on a beautiful pair of these. Plants use sunlight to drive an endothermic reaction, photosynthesis, and store the energy in sugar. You run the same reaction in reverse, respiration, which releases that energy to power your heartbeat and your thoughts. Follow Cass to see the whole cycle.

Lab 9 · Follow one atom

Cass's journey

Macro

Everyday miracle

You breathe out your breakfast

When you burn fat, where do its atoms go? A 2014 study worked it out: about 84% leaves your body as carbon dioxide through your lungs. The rest leaves as water.

That fog on the cold window this morning? Water and carbon dioxide made from food you ate yesterday, assembled by a plant from air and sunshine some months before.

The reveal

Who did it?

Nine clues. Nine everyday mysteries. Lay them side by side, and ask the question every detective asks: who was present at every scene?

The culprit is

The electron

And its accomplice: the simple fact that opposite charges attract and like charges repel.

Electrons hold the crowd of molecules together, and their attractions decide whether something is solid, liquid or gas. Electron clouds stop your hand going through the table. The outer electrons give every family in the periodic table its personality. Bonds are electrons given or shared. Water is sticky because Ola hogs the electrons. Reactions are electrons rearranging, and they only happen when collisions are hard enough to shift them. Acids work by handing over a proton left behind when an electron is gone. And the energy of life is stored in, and released from, the arrangement of electrons in sugar.

The nucleus decides what an atom is. The electrons decide what it does. Almost everything you will ever learn in chemistry is a new chapter in the electron's story. Case closed… for now.
